01

Overview

Two Franka Panda manipulators transfer a cube between platforms on separate tables. Neither arm can complete the full task alone: they must meet in a shared workspace and exchange the object in mid-air.

Developed for Robotics II in Brussels, this simulation project brings together workspace analysis, inverse kinematics, motion planning and task coordination.

02

Requirements & constraints

The exchange pose must be reachable by both seven-degree-of-freedom arms. Their concurrent approach must avoid collisions with the environment and each other.

The transfer sequence must give the receiving robot ownership of the cube before the first robot releases it. A shared state machine coordinates these events.

03

My contribution

I developed the complete project independently: workspace analysis, inverse kinematics, motion planning, gripper ownership logic, task coordination and simulation integration.

04

Approach & decisions

A hierarchical architecture separates coordination, planning and control. A central finite-state machine synchronizes the task; each arm has its own controller.

OMPL planning is reserved for the critical approach to the exchange pose. Pick and place motions in the open workspace use simpler trajectories, reducing planning work.

The handover configuration is computed offline through workspace analysis and loaded at runtime. This favors repeatability and fast execution over online adaptation.

After contact-based grasp simulation proved unstable, I used a kinematic attachment: the cube follows the owning gripper and ownership changes during the exchange. State transitions are based on trajectory completion.

05

Results & validation

The January 2026 final presentation reports a completed pick–handover–place sequence in approximately 20 seconds, with a final placement error of 9.1 mm.

The demonstrated sequence contains 13 states. Validation covered one complete run in the nominal configuration, with the cube initially at [0.5, 0.5, 0.55] and standard controller gains.

06

Limitations & next steps

These are proof-of-concept results from a single nominal simulation, not statistics over repeated trials or physical robot measurements.

The kinematic grasp cannot validate slip, grasp failure or force-based transfer. The exchange pose is fixed, and trajectory completion does not independently verify actual proximity.

A stronger evaluation would vary initial object positions, introduce disturbances and test contact-based grasping over repeated runs.
