Apple Watch case: production study

01

Geometry and tolerances

Only the external surfaces are public, so we inferred the internals — battery cavity, crown and button seats, microphone and strap slots — from teardown photos, then assigned tolerances by function: ±0.01 mm on precision fits, ±0.1 mm on structural walls, and a Ø0.05 mm concentricity on the Ø3 H7 crown bore.

02

Process route

  • Blanking: a 3000 × 1500 × 10 mm Al 7005-T6 sheet is laser-cut into three 1500 × 1000 mm panels (AMADA REGIUS fibre laser, chosen on cutting speed and bed size).
  • Panel machining: the panel is located on Ø6 H7 reference holes, faced, and the internal cavities of all 608 blanks are milled from both sides before the blanks are cut out.
  • Single-part machining: external surfaces on a DATRON MLCube, whose 60,000 rpm spindle is what the 1 mm finishing tools need in aluminium.
  • Finishing: vibratory tumbling with low-aggressiveness plastic media, from Ra 0.8 to the Ra 0.4 µm target without rounding the edges.

03

Capacity, tooling and cost

For every operation we computed removed volume, material-removal rate and cutting power, and from those the machining time and energy. Tool life came from Taylor's law (literature values for carbide on aluminium, n = 0.25, C = 900 m/min), which set how many parts each tool makes before it's changed.

Sizing the line for 1,000,000 parts a year gave 1 laser cutter, 89 CNC machining centres and 1 tumbling machine. Cavity milling turned out to be the bottleneck, so instead of buying more machines we split the year into four periods and reassigned the fleet between phases. A cost model rolls machine amortisation, maintenance, energy and tooling into a cost per part.

04

Scope

An academic study of a public product, not work for Apple: internal geometry, tolerances, machine prices and tool-life constants are our assumptions and were not validated with machining trials.
